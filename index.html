📋 Etiology Deep Dives

Septic Shock (62% of all shock)

Definition (Sepsis-3): Sepsis with persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors for MAP ≥65 mmHg AND lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate volume resuscitation. [1][5]

Common Sources:

  • Pulmonary — pneumonia (most common), empyema
  • Abdominal — peritonitis, cholangitis, abscess, ischemic bowel
  • Urinary — pyelonephritis, obstructed infected system
  • Skin/Soft tissue — necrotizing fasciitis, cellulitis
  • Line-related — CLABSI

Management: Early broad-spectrum antibiotics (within 1hr) + source control → IV crystalloid → norepinephrine → vasopressin → hydrocortisone if refractory. [5][2]

Anaphylactic Shock

Pathophysiology: IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation → massive histamine release → vasodilation + bronchospasm + capillary leak + mucosal edema. [5]

Clinical Features: Rapid onset after exposure, urticaria/angioedema (~90%), bronchospasm/stridor, hypotension, GI symptoms.

Common Triggers: Antibiotics, NSAIDs, NMBAs, foods, insect stings, latex, contrast, blood products.

Management: STOP offending agent → IM Epinephrine 0.3–0.5mg (repeat q5–15min) → aggressive IV fluids → secure airway → adjuncts (H1/H2 blockers, steroids, albuterol). [5]

⚠️ Epinephrine is the ONLY first-line treatment. Do NOT substitute antihistamines or steroids.
Neurogenic Shock

Pathophysiology: Disruption of descending sympathetic pathways (cervical/high thoracic SCI, T6 and above) → loss of vascular tone + loss of cardiac accelerator fibers → vasodilation + bradycardia. [5]

Distinguishing Features: Hypotension + bradycardia (unlike all other shock types); warm, flushed skin below level; poikilothermia.

Management: Spinal stabilization → Target MAP 85–90 mmHg (spinal cord perfusion) → Norepinephrine or phenylephrine → Atropine for symptomatic bradycardia. [5]

⚠️ Rule out hemorrhage. Trauma patients may have concurrent hemorrhagic shock — do not attribute all hypotension to neurogenic cause.
Adrenal Crisis

Pathophysiology: Inadequate cortisol response to stress → impaired vascular tone + decreased catecholamine sensitivity. Most common ICU cause: chronic steroid suppression. [2][5]

Clues: Steroid history, refractory hypotension despite fluids + vasopressors, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, eosinophilia.

Management: IV Hydrocortisone 200mg/day (50mg q6h). Often dramatic improvement in vasopressor responsiveness within hours. [2][5]

Post-CPB Vasoplegia

Pathophysiology: Contact activation with bypass circuit → systemic inflammatory response → NO-mediated vasoplegia. Incidence 5–25% post-CPB. [9]

Management: Norepinephrine → Vasopressin → Methylene blue (inhibits NO/guanylate cyclase) → Hydroxocobalamin (NO scavenger). [9]

Drug-Induced Distributive Shock

Culprits: Calcium channel blockers, sedatives/anesthetics (propofol, volatiles), vasodilators (nitroprusside, NTG), ACEi/ARB, cytokine release syndrome (post CAR-T).

Key Principle: Identify and discontinue offending agent. Supportive vasopressor therapy. For CCB OD: high-dose insulin, IV calcium, lipid emulsion.

🔬 Pathophysiology of Distributive Shock

The primary defect is in the periphery — inappropriate vasodilation with maldistribution of blood flow, decreased SVR, and impaired oxygen utilization despite preserved or elevated cardiac output. [1]

1
🔥 Inflammatory Trigger
Infection/injury activates immune response → proinflammatory mediators enter systemic circulation → vasoplegia and organ dysfunction. [1][2]
2
🧬 Mediator Cascade
Cytokines: TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6 drive endothelial activation, capillary leak, microcirculatory dysfunction, and coagulation activation. [2]
Nitric Oxide: iNOS upregulation → excessive NO → guanylate cyclase activation → smooth muscle relaxation. Acidosis opens K-ATP channels → membrane hyperpolarization → catecholamine resistance. [5]
3
🫀 Vascular Consequences
Vasodilation
Decreased SVR, decreased MAP, loss of vascular tone [1]
Relative Hypovolemia
Venous pooling, decreased effective circulating volume, decreased venous return [1][6]
Capillary Leak
Glycocalyx injury, plasma extravasation, interstitial edema, impaired O2 diffusion [1][5]
Microcirculatory Failure
Decreased capillary density, flow heterogeneity, shunting past exchange vessels [4]
4
💔 Myocardial Depression (Late)
Circulating depressant factors (TNF-alpha, IL-1-beta, NO) → biventricular dilation with reduced EF. Often reversible. Contributes to "cold"/late septic shock phenotype. [5]
⚠️ Key Insight [1]: The sympathetic response to distributive shock mirrors "running from a bear" — Alpha-1 shunts blood from viscera/skin to muscles, Beta-1 augments CO, Beta-2 bronchodilates and releases lactate as fuel. In septic shock, these compensatory mechanisms become overwhelmed by the inflammatory cascade.

Early vs. Late Septic Shock

Early ("Warm"): Hyperdynamic — ↑CO, ↓↓SVR, warm extremities, bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure. SvO₂ elevated (impaired extraction). [5][6]
Late ("Cold"): Myocardial depression → ↓CO, mottled skin, poor cap refill, narrow pulse pressure. Worse prognosis. [5]
⚠️ Pearl: "Warm" + low SVR + preserved CO = distributive. "Cold" + low CO may be cardiogenic, hypovolemic, OR late distributive — echo differentiates.

💊 Medical & Pharmacologic Management

Initial Resuscitation

Volume Resuscitation
IV crystalloid — individualize based on clinical response. The SSC recommends 30 mL/kg within 3 hours (weak recommendation), but ARISE FLUIDS (2026) found no outcome difference when this volume was not completed. Assess fluid responsiveness before additional boluses. Excess fluid worsens outcomes. [2][4][5]
Vasopressor Timing
Early vasopressor initiation is safe — even via peripheral IV (71.9% received peripherally in ARISE FLUIDS). Does not require central access or ICU admission to start. Median time to vasopressor cessation was shorter with early initiation (22.4h vs. 30.1h). [2]
Source Control
Drain abscesses, debride necrotic tissue, remove infected devices, repair perforations. Every hour of delay matters. [5]
Antimicrobials
Empiric broad-spectrum within 1 hour. Cultures first if possible without delaying. De-escalate on results. [5][2]

Escalation Pathway [2][5]

Mark current step:

STEP 1Norepinephrine (First-Line)
"We consider norepinephrine the vasopressor of first choice" [1]. Target MAP 65 mmHg or higher. Potent Alpha-1 vasoconstriction + mild Beta-1 inotropy. Superior to dopamine (SOAP II), equivalent to epinephrine. [1][4]
STEP 2Vasopressin (0.01-0.04 U/min)
Non-catecholamine; V1 receptors. Catecholamine-sparing. Posterior pituitary stores deplete in prolonged shock — hormone replacement rationale. [1][5]
Do not exceed 0.04 U/min. Do not titrate to MAP. [1]
STEP 3Adjuncts: AngII / Epinephrine / Hydrocortisone
AngII: ATHOS-3. Epi: Refractory/inotropy needed — strong Beta-1 for chronotropy and contractility [1]. Hydrocortisone 200mg/d: If 2+ vasopressors or escalating doses — endogenous cortisol stores may be inadequate for prolonged stress response [1].
Methylene Blue: Refractory vasoplegia (post-CPB, late septic).
DE-ESCALATIONWeaning Vasopressors
Wean adjuncts first → vasopressin last (or keep fixed until NE low) → NE last. Target negative fluid balance. Signal: MAP at target on stable/decreasing doses, lactate normalizing, UOP improving. [1][4]

🔍 Assessment & Monitoring

Continuous feedback loop: assess → intervene → reassess → adjust. [1][4]

🕐 First 1–3 Hours: Stabilize & Identify

🎯 MAP Target
MAP 65 or higher if chronic HTN. At what pressor dose? What is the trajectory? [4]
💧 Fluid Response
After 30 mL/kg: PLR (spont breathing), PPV/SVV (vented), or mini-challenge. Responsive = more fluid. Not = pressors. [4]
🔍 Source ID
Cultures before abx (if obtainable in minutes). Imaging PRN. Assess surgical source control needs. [5]

Lactate trend, UOP, pressor doses/NEE, perfusion windows, fluid balance, reassess fluid responsiveness before boluses. [4]

Core Principle: Never determine readiness by an isolated number. Assess trends and trajectories — a single snapshot is insufficient. A patient on NE 0.15 trending down is fundamentally different from NE 0.08 trending up. [6]

🔬 Pathophysiology of Cardiogenic Shock

The primary defect is the pump. Inadequate cardiac output despite adequate intravascular volume → sustained tissue hypoperfusion → a self-reinforcing spiral of ischemia, congestion, and systemic decompensation. [3][5][9]

1
💔 Primary Pump Failure
Ischemic (AMI, most common), mechanical, arrhythmic, or end-stage myopathic insult → acute drop in contractility and stroke volume → ↓cardiac output. [5][9]
2
🧬 Compensatory Neurohormonal Activation
Sympathetic surge + RAAS activation: Catecholamines raise HR and contractility; angiotensin II and aldosterone drive vasoconstriction and sodium/water retention. Initially protective, these mechanisms raise afterload and myocardial O₂ demand. [3][9]
The double-edged sword: Vasoconstriction maintains MAP but increases the failing ventricle's workload, worsening the supply-demand mismatch. [3]
3
🫀 Hemodynamic Consequences
Forward Failure
↓CO, ↓MAP, ↓coronary perfusion pressure → end-organ hypoperfusion [3][9]
Backward Failure
↑LVEDP → ↑PCWP → pulmonary congestion, hypoxemia [3]
Venous Congestion
↑CVP → congestive hepatic/renal injury — raises mortality independent of CO [3]
↑Myocardial O₂ Demand
Tachycardia + ↑afterload + wall stress widen the supply-demand gap [3][9]
4
🌀 The Downward Spiral
↓Coronary perfusion → further ischemia → further ↓contractility → further ↓CO. Without interruption (revascularization, inotropy, or mechanical support), the cycle is self-perpetuating and lethal. [3][9]
5
🔥 Mixed Shock Phenotype (Late, ~20–25%)
Prolonged hypoperfusion triggers a SIRS-like inflammatory response → iNOS upregulation → inappropriate vasodilation superimposed on low CO. SVR falls paradoxically. PAC helpful to characterize; may require methylene blue or hydroxocobalamin. [9]
Cardiac Power Output: CPO = (MAP × CO) ÷ 451
The single strongest hemodynamic predictor of in-hospital mortality (SHOCK trial). CPO <0.6 W denotes severe pump failure. [3][9]
⚠️ Key Insight: Cardiogenic shock is fundamentally a problem of afterload mismatch and a supply-demand spiral — not volume depletion. Reflexive fluid loading often worsens congestion. The therapeutic goal is to break the spiral: restore coronary flow, support contractility, and offload the ventricle. [3][9]

📊 SCAI Staging & Etiologies

The SCAI SHOCK classification stages severity from "At Risk" (A) to "Extremis" (E). Staging is dynamic — patients move between stages, and the modifier of cardiac arrest worsens prognosis at every stage. [9]

Select a stage:

STAGE AAt Risk~3% mortality
No current signs of shock but at risk (e.g., large AMI, decompensating HF). Normal JVP, clear lungs, warm and well-perfused.
Labs: normal lactate, normal renal function. Hemodynamics: normal.
STAGE BBeginning (Pre-Shock / Compensated)~7% mortality
Hemodynamic instability without hypoperfusion. Tachycardia and/or hypotension (SBP <90 or MAP <60) but perfusion still preserved — normal lactate.
Exam: elevated JVP, rales possible. Labs: normal lactate, minimal renal change.
STAGE CClassic Cardiogenic Shock~12% mortality
Hypoperfusion requiring intervention (inotrope, pressor, or mechanical support) beyond volume. The "textbook" presentation: cool, clammy, oliguric, altered.
Labs: lactate ≥2, rising creatinine, ↑BNP. Hemodynamics: CI <2.2, PCWP >15, CPO ≤0.6 W.
STAGE DDeteriorating / Doom~40% mortality
Failure to respond to initial interventions — escalating support required. Not stabilizing on the current strategy.
Labs: lactate rising or >2 and not clearing, worsening organ dysfunction. Requires escalation of pharmacology or tMCS.
STAGE EExtremis~67% mortality
Circulatory collapse, often refractory cardiac arrest or requiring CPR/ECMO. Profound, multi-system hypoperfusion.
Labs: severe lactic acidosis (often >8), pH <7.2. Hemodynamics: profound hypotension despite maximal support; near-PEA physiology.
⚠️ Cardiac Arrest Modifier (A): The presence of cardiac arrest at any stage independently worsens prognosis and is appended as a modifier (e.g., "C-A"). [9]

📋 Etiology Deep Dives

AMI-CS (Acute MI — most common)

Mechanism: Loss of contractile mass (typically >40% LV) or mechanical complication. Worst prognosis of the etiologies (~30% in-hospital mortality). [9]

Priority: Emergent revascularization of the culprit vessel. [9]

⚠️ CULPRIT-SHOCK (NEJM 2017): In multivessel disease with AMI-CS, culprit-lesion-only PCI was superior to immediate multivessel PCI (lower 30-day death/RRT). Citation flagged — verify year/numbers against the primary source.
HF-CS (Acute-on-Chronic Heart Failure)

Mechanism: Decompensation of chronic cardiomyopathy. Often more congestion-predominant with relatively preserved adaptation. Mortality ~25%. [9]

Priority: Decongestion, inotropic support, evaluation for durable therapies (LVAD/transplant) in appropriate candidates. [9]

Mechanical Complications

Includes: Acute mitral regurgitation (papillary muscle rupture), ventricular septal rupture, free wall rupture/tamponade. [9]

Priority: Urgent echo to identify; surgical or transcatheter repair. tMCS as bridge. High index of suspicion in AMI-CS that decompensates abruptly.

RV-Predominant Cardiogenic Shock

Mechanism: RV infarction (inferior MI), acute PE, or RV failure post-LVAD. Preload-dependent and afterload-sensitive physiology. [9]

Priority: Maintain RV preload (cautious volume), reduce PVR, support RV contractility, avoid hypoxemia/hypercarbia/acidosis. RV-specific MCS (e.g., RV Impella, VA-ECMO) if refractory.

Arrhythmic

Mechanism: Sustained tachy- or brady-arrhythmia compromising CO. Priority: Restore rhythm/rate — cardioversion, antiarrhythmics, or pacing. Often rapidly reversible once rhythm corrected.

Valvular & Post-Cardiotomy

Valvular: Acute severe AR/MR or critical AS/MS. Post-cardiotomy shock: Failure to wean from bypass or early postoperative pump failure. [9]

Priority: Address the structural lesion; tMCS frequently required as a bridge to recovery or definitive repair.

💊 Medical & Pharmacologic Management

Initial Stabilization

Diagnose the Phenotype
Immediate bedside echo + 12-lead ECG. Identify AMI vs. HF-CS vs. mechanical vs. RV-predominant. Consider early PAC to characterize CI, PCWP, CVP, and CPO — particularly when phenotype is unclear or mixed shock suspected. [3][9]
Restore Coronary Flow
For AMI-CS: emergent revascularization of the culprit lesion is the single most important intervention. Do not delay for hemodynamic "optimization." [9]
Volume — Carefully
Unlike other shock states, most CS patients are NOT volume depleted. Reserve cautious 250–500 mL challenges for the rare under-filled or RV-predominant patient; assess response. Diurese/decongest if PCWP/CVP elevated. [3][5]
Oxygenation & Ventilation
Treat hypoxemia. Positive-pressure ventilation reduces LV afterload and work of breathing but can impair RV preload — titrate carefully, especially in RV-predominant CS. [9]
⚠️ Mantra: "Open the artery, support the pump, offload the ventricle." Pressors and inotropes are a bridge — they buy time for revascularization or mechanical support, not a destination. Every µg of catecholamine raises myocardial O₂ demand. [3][9]

Escalation Pathway [3][5][9]

Mark current step:

STEP 1Diagnose & Optimize
Echo + ECG, treat the cause (revascularize AMI, correct arrhythmia, address mechanical lesion). Optimize preload (decongest if wet, cautious volume if RV/dry), correct hypoxemia/acidosis. Establish CI, PCWP, CPO baseline. [3][9]
STEP 2Inotrope ± Vasopressor
Norepinephrine to restore MAP/coronary perfusion + dobutamine or milrinone for inotropy. Use the lowest effective doses — catecholamines raise myocardial O₂ demand and are a bridge, not a destination. Reassess CI/CPO and perfusion. [4][5][9]
Escalating doses or rising lactate = SCAI D → do not delay mechanical support evaluation.
STEP 3Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support
If pharmacologic support is inadequate (persistent hypoperfusion, CPO ≤0.6 W, rising lactate), escalate early to tMCS rather than maximizing catecholamines. Device choice depends on phenotype (LV vs. RV vs. biventricular, oxygenation needs). See tMCS tab. [3][9]
Early tMCS (before profound multi-organ failure) is associated with better outcomes — escalate at SCAI C→D, not E.
DE-ESCALATIONWeaning Support
As contractility recovers (improving CI/CPO, clearing lactate, rising UOP): wean catecholamines first, then wean/decommission tMCS per device protocol with echo guidance. Transition to oral GDMT for HF-CS. [5][9]

🔧 Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support

tMCS supports circulation while the cause is addressed (bridge to recovery, decision, or durable therapy). Device selection is driven by the failing ventricle(s), oxygenation needs, and the degree of support required. [3][9]

🎯 Device Selection Logic

Isolated LV failure, no oxygenation problem
Impella (CP or 5.5) provides direct LV unloading without the afterload penalty of VA-ECMO. IABP if only modest support needed and Impella unavailable. [3][9]
Biventricular failure or respiratory failure
VA-ECMO provides full cardiopulmonary support and oxygenation. Plan for LV venting (ECPELLA or IABP) if LV distension/pulmonary edema develops. [3][9]
Isolated/predominant RV failure
RV-specific support (Impella RP, ProtekDuo) or VA-ECMO. Avoid raising PVR; optimize RV preload and contractility. [9]
Timing
Escalate early (SCAI C→D) before profound multi-organ failure. Outcomes are better when tMCS precedes irreversible end-organ injury rather than as a salvage maneuver at SCAI E. [9]
LANDMARK TRIAL DanGer Shock (2024)

First adequately powered RCT of a microaxial flow pump (Impella CP) vs. standard care in STEMI-related cardiogenic shock. [9]

↓ 180-day mortality
Significant reduction vs. standard care in STEMI-CS [9]
↑ Adverse events
More limb ischemia, bleeding, hemolysis, renal replacement [9]

💪 Rehab Considerations

Core Principle: As in all shock, readiness is a trajectory — not a single number. In cardiogenic shock the added layer is mechanical support: a patient on a stable, weaning Impella with clearing lactate is fundamentally different from one on escalating inotropes. Device platform and access site constrain mobility. [5][6]

🔬 Pathophysiology

Physical impediment to blood flow. CO↓ due to mechanical obstruction. Rarest (~2%). [5][6]

Hemodynamics
↓CO, ↑SVR, ↑CVP, variable PCWP, ↓SvO₂, cool extremities. [5]
PE Mechanism
≥50% vascular bed → acute ↑RV afterload → RV dilation → septal shift → ↓LV filling → ↓CO. [5][6]

🔍 Etiologies & Treatment

Tension Pneumothorax

Dx: Clinical — absent lung sliding on POCUS. Tx: Needle decompression → thoracostomy. [5]

Cardiac Tamponade

Dx: Beck's triad, pulsus paradoxus. Echo: RV diastolic collapse. Tx: Pericardiocentesis. [5]

Massive PE

Dx: SBP<90 >15min. RV dilation, McConnell's sign. Tx: Thrombolysis, CDT, embolectomy, or VA-ECMO. [5][6]
