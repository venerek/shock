Shock Management Tool

🫀 Shock Management Tool

🔬 Pathophysiology of Distributive Shock

The primary defect is in the periphery — inappropriate vasodilation with maldistribution of blood flow, decreased SVR, and impaired oxygen utilization despite preserved or elevated cardiac output. [1]

1
🔥 Inflammatory Trigger
Infection/injury activates immune response → proinflammatory mediators enter systemic circulation → vasoplegia and organ dysfunction. [1][2]
2
🧬 Mediator Cascade
Cytokines: TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6 drive endothelial activation, capillary leak, microcirculatory dysfunction, and coagulation activation. [2]
Nitric Oxide: iNOS upregulation → excessive NO → guanylate cyclase activation → smooth muscle relaxation. Acidosis opens K-ATP channels → membrane hyperpolarization → catecholamine resistance. [5]
3
🫀 Vascular Consequences
Vasodilation
Decreased SVR, decreased MAP, loss of vascular tone [1]
Relative Hypovolemia
Venous pooling, decreased effective circulating volume, decreased venous return [1][6]
Capillary Leak
Glycocalyx injury, plasma extravasation, interstitial edema, impaired O2 diffusion [1][5]
Microcirculatory Failure
Decreased capillary density, flow heterogeneity, shunting past exchange vessels [4]
4
💔 Myocardial Depression (Late)
Circulating depressant factors (TNF-alpha, IL-1-beta, NO) → biventricular dilation with reduced EF. Often reversible. Contributes to "cold"/late septic shock phenotype. [5]
⚠️ Key Insight [1]: The sympathetic response to distributive shock mirrors "running from a bear" — Alpha-1 shunts blood from viscera/skin to muscles, Beta-1 augments CO, Beta-2 bronchodilates and releases lactate as fuel. In septic shock, these compensatory mechanisms become overwhelmed by the inflammatory cascade.

🦠 Etiologies of Distributive Shock

Distributive shock accounts for the majority (~66%) of shock in ICU. Identifying the specific etiology directly determines management. [1][5]

📋 Etiology Deep Dives

Septic Shock (62% of all shock)

Definition (Sepsis-3): Sepsis with persisting hypotension requiring vasopressors for MAP ≥65 mmHg AND lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate volume resuscitation. [1][5]

Common Sources:

  • Pulmonary — pneumonia (most common), empyema
  • Abdominal — peritonitis, cholangitis, abscess, ischemic bowel
  • Urinary — pyelonephritis, obstructed infected system
  • Skin/Soft tissue — necrotizing fasciitis, cellulitis
  • Line-related — CLABSI

Management: Early broad-spectrum antibiotics (within 1hr) + source control → IV crystalloid → norepinephrine → vasopressin → hydrocortisone if refractory. [5][2]

Anaphylactic Shock

Pathophysiology: IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation → massive histamine release → vasodilation + bronchospasm + capillary leak + mucosal edema. [5]

Clinical Features: Rapid onset after exposure, urticaria/angioedema (~90%), bronchospasm/stridor, hypotension, GI symptoms.

Common Triggers: Antibiotics, NSAIDs, NMBAs, foods, insect stings, latex, contrast, blood products.

Management: STOP offending agent → IM Epinephrine 0.3–0.5mg (repeat q5–15min) → aggressive IV fluids → secure airway → adjuncts (H1/H2 blockers, steroids, albuterol). [5]

⚠️ Epinephrine is the ONLY first-line treatment. Do NOT substitute antihistamines or steroids.
Neurogenic Shock

Pathophysiology: Disruption of descending sympathetic pathways (cervical/high thoracic SCI, T6 and above) → loss of vascular tone + loss of cardiac accelerator fibers → vasodilation + bradycardia. [5]

Distinguishing Features: Hypotension + bradycardia (unlike all other shock types); warm, flushed skin below level; poikilothermia.

Management: Spinal stabilization → Target MAP 85–90 mmHg (spinal cord perfusion) → Norepinephrine or phenylephrine → Atropine for symptomatic bradycardia. [5]

⚠️ Rule out hemorrhage. Trauma patients may have concurrent hemorrhagic shock — do not attribute all hypotension to neurogenic cause.
Adrenal Crisis

Pathophysiology: Inadequate cortisol response to stress → impaired vascular tone + decreased catecholamine sensitivity. Most common ICU cause: chronic steroid suppression. [2][5]

Clues: Steroid history, refractory hypotension despite fluids + vasopressors, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, eosinophilia.

Management: IV Hydrocortisone 200mg/day (50mg q6h). Often dramatic improvement in vasopressor responsiveness within hours. [2][5]

Post-CPB Vasoplegia

Pathophysiology: Contact activation with bypass circuit → systemic inflammatory response → NO-mediated vasoplegia. Incidence 5–25% post-CPB. [9]

Management: Norepinephrine → Vasopressin → Methylene blue (inhibits NO/guanylate cyclase) → Hydroxocobalamin (NO scavenger). [9]

Drug-Induced Distributive Shock

Culprits: Calcium channel blockers, sedatives/anesthetics (propofol, volatiles), vasodilators (nitroprusside, NTG), ACEi/ARB, cytokine release syndrome (post CAR-T).

Key Principle: Identify and discontinue offending agent. Supportive vasopressor therapy. For CCB OD: high-dose insulin, IV calcium, lipid emulsion.

Early vs. Late Septic Shock

Early ("Warm"): Hyperdynamic — ↑CO, ↓↓SVR, warm extremities, bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure. SvO₂ elevated (impaired extraction). [5][6]
Late ("Cold"): Myocardial depression → ↓CO, mottled skin, poor cap refill, narrow pulse pressure. Worse prognosis. [5]
⚠️ Pearl: "Warm" + low SVR + preserved CO = distributive. "Cold" + low CO may be cardiogenic, hypovolemic, OR late distributive — echo differentiates.

💊 Medical & Pharmacologic Management

Initial Resuscitation

Volume Resuscitation
IV crystalloid — individualize based on clinical response. The SSC recommends 30 mL/kg within 3 hours (weak recommendation), but ARISE FLUIDS (2026) found no outcome difference when this volume was not completed. Assess fluid responsiveness before additional boluses. Excess fluid worsens outcomes. [2][4][5]
Vasopressor Timing
Early vasopressor initiation is safe — even via peripheral IV (71.9% received peripherally in ARISE FLUIDS). Does not require central access or ICU admission to start. Median time to vasopressor cessation was shorter with early initiation (22.4h vs. 30.1h). [2]
Source Control
Drain abscesses, debride necrotic tissue, remove infected devices, repair perforations. Every hour of delay matters. [5]
Antimicrobials
Empiric broad-spectrum within 1 hour. Cultures first if possible without delaying. De-escalate on results. [5][2]

Escalation Pathway [2][5]

Mark current step:

STEP 1Norepinephrine (First-Line)
"We consider norepinephrine the vasopressor of first choice" [1]. Target MAP 65 mmHg or higher. Potent Alpha-1 vasoconstriction + mild Beta-1 inotropy. Superior to dopamine (SOAP II), equivalent to epinephrine. [1][4]
STEP 2Vasopressin (0.01-0.04 U/min)
Non-catecholamine; V1 receptors. Catecholamine-sparing. Posterior pituitary stores deplete in prolonged shock — hormone replacement rationale. [1][5]
Do not exceed 0.04 U/min. Do not titrate to MAP. [1]
STEP 3Adjuncts: AngII / Epinephrine / Hydrocortisone
AngII: ATHOS-3. Epi: Refractory/inotropy needed — strong Beta-1 for chronotropy and contractility [1]. Hydrocortisone 200mg/d: If 2+ vasopressors or escalating doses — endogenous cortisol stores may be inadequate for prolonged stress response [1].
Methylene Blue: Refractory vasoplegia (post-CPB, late septic).
DE-ESCALATIONWeaning Vasopressors
Wean adjuncts first → vasopressin last (or keep fixed until NE low) → NE last. Target negative fluid balance. Signal: MAP at target on stable/decreasing doses, lactate normalizing, UOP improving. [1][4]

🔍 Assessment & Monitoring

Continuous feedback loop: assess → intervene → reassess → adjust. [1][4]

🕐 First 1–3 Hours: Stabilize & Identify

🎯 MAP Target
MAP 65 or higher if chronic HTN. At what pressor dose? What is the trajectory? [4]
💧 Fluid Response
After 30 mL/kg: PLR (spont breathing), PPV/SVV (vented), or mini-challenge. Responsive = more fluid. Not = pressors. [4]
🔍 Source ID
Cultures before abx (if obtainable in minutes). Imaging PRN. Assess surgical source control needs. [5]

🔄 Ongoing & Reassessment Triggers

🕐 Routine (q2-6h)
Lactate trend, UOP, pressor doses/NEE, perfusion windows, fluid balance, reassess fluid responsiveness before boluses. [4]
⚠️ Escalate If
New or rising pressor need, rising lactate, new organ dysfunction, failure to improve at 6-12h.
Ask: Inadequate source control? New infection? Bleeding? Adrenal insufficiency? Wrong shock type?
✅ De-Resuscitation Signals
MAP at target on stable or decreasing pressors, lactate normalizing, UOP adequate, no longer fluid responsive → target negative balance (Phase 4). [1][4]

Core Principle: Never determine readiness by an isolated number. Assess trends and trajectories — a single snapshot is insufficient. A patient on NE 0.15 trending down is fundamentally different from NE 0.08 trending up. [6]

🔬 Pathophysiology of Cardiogenic Shock

A low-output state due to primary cardiac dysfunction leading to systemic decompensation, end-organ dysfunction, and secondary myocardial injury. [1]

1
💔 Primary Cardiac Dysfunction
Most commonly acute coronary syndrome with LV dysfunction (up to 81% of cases), followed by acute decompensated heart failure. SBP <80–90 mmHg, CI <1.8 L/min/m² without support (<2.2 with support), plus signs of tissue hypoperfusion. [1]
2
🫀 Compensatory Vasoconstriction
Compensatory peripheral vasoconstriction raises afterload — often poorly tolerated by a struggling pump. This produces the "cold and wet" profile (cool extremities + pulmonary congestion from LV failure). [1]
3
🔥 Underappreciated Inflammatory Response
A systemic inflammatory response can predominate with peripheral vasodilation — described as "wet and warm." Septic cardiomyopathy can produce a mixed-shock state, better elucidated with a PAC. [1]
4
💀 Systemic Decompensation
End-organ hypoperfusion and secondary myocardial injury perpetuate the low-output state. Mortality remains high at 40–67%. [1]
⚠️ RV vs. LV failure [1]: The presence and degree of RV and LV failure affects clinical presentation and determines management strategy (type of inotropic support, afterload reduction, diuretic use). Identify early.

📊 SCAI Staging & Etiologies

The SCAI classification standardizes the definition and severity of cardiogenic shock for timely risk stratification and intervention. Staging is dynamic — patients move between stages. [1]

Select a stage:

STAGE AAt Risk
At risk for CS (large acute MI or prior infarct, worsening HF symptoms). Normal CVP, no rales, warm, good peripheral pulses, normal mentation. [1]
Labs: normal lactate & renal function. Hemodynamics: SBP >100 or normal; if measured, CI ≥2.5, CVP <10, ScvO₂ ≥65%. [1]
STAGE BBeginning CS
Clinical evidence of hypotension or tachycardia without hypoperfusion. Increased CVP, good peripheral pulses, normal mentation. [1]
Labs: minimal renal impairment, elevated BNP. Hemodynamics: SBP <90 or MAP <60 or >30 drop, HR ≥100; if done CI ≥2.2, ScvO₂ ≥65%. [1]
STAGE CClassic CS
Hypoperfusion requiring intervention (pharmacologic or temporary MCS), typically with hypotension. May look unwell, panicked, ashen, volume overloaded, rales, Killip 3–4, cold/clammy, altered MS, decreased UO. [1]
Labs: lactate >2, doubling creatinine or 50% GFR drop, ↑LFTs/BNP. Hemodynamics: CI <2.2, Ppao ≥15, CPO ≤0.6. [1]
STAGE DDeteriorating / Doom
Not responding to initial interventions in Stage C. Any of Stage C and deteriorating. [1]
Requires multiple pressors and/or mechanical circulatory support to maintain flow. [1]
STAGE EExtremis
Near pulselessness, cardiac collapse, or CPR — with or without ECMO, mechanical ventilation, pacing. "Trying to die." [1]
Labs: pH ≤7.2, lactate ≥5. Hemodynamics: no SBP without resuscitation, PEA, or refractory VT/VF despite maximal support. [1]
⚠️ Mortality rises steeply with stage; overall CS mortality is 40–67%. [1]

📋 Etiology Deep Dives

Ischemic (most common — up to 81%)

Includes: Acute MI secondary to LV or RV infarct; mechanical complication of ischemic infarct. ACS with resultant LV dysfunction is the leading cause. [1]

Priority: If ACS is present, emergent coronary angiography and revascularization is the mainstay — with demonstrated mortality benefit across racial/ethnic subgroups. [1]

Nonischemic — Acute / Acute-on-Chronic HF

Includes: Acute decompensated heart failure (chronic ischemia, dilated cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, stress-induced/Takotsubo, peripartum cardiomyopathy), second most common cause after ACS. [1]

Other Etiologies

Includes: Post-cardiotomy CS, post-cardiac-arrest myocardial stunning, septic cardiomyopathy, myocardial contusion, and dynamic LVOT obstruction (hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, hypertensive cardiomyopathy, Takotsubo). [1]

💊 Medical & Pharmacologic Management

Initial Stabilization

Diagnose & Revascularize
Serial troponins + ECG are essential. If STEMI or ACS changes are present, emergent coronary angiography and revascularization is the mainstay — mortality benefit demonstrated. [1]
Targets
Keep cardiac index >2.2 L/min/m² and MAP >65 mmHg. RA and PA wedge pressures are poor predictors of fluid response, but trends with echo, exam, and biomarkers may be useful. [1]
Volume — Judicious
If indicated, judicious 250–500 mL challenges avoid overstressing the failing pump. Conversely, furosemide ± thiazide if congestion/total body volume elevated. [1]
Respiratory Support
Noninvasive PPV often used first and may avoid intubation. Moderate–high PEEP (5–15 cm H₂O) may improve CO in afterload-dependent LV failure. Low tidal volume (4–6 mL/kg) is lung-protective; minimize sedation and increase mobility. [1]
⚠️ Avoid negative inotropes (e.g., beta-blockers). Consider chemical/electrical pacing if bradyarrhythmias contribute to hypoperfusion; urgent cardioversion for new-onset tachyarrhythmias (e.g., atrial fibrillation). [1]

Inotrope & Vasopressor Pharmacology

Catecholamine Caution
Catecholamines may transiently improve hemodynamics but are associated with worse long-term outcomes in HFrEF — likely from arrhythmogenicity and increased myocardial O₂ consumption. [1]
Dobutamine vs. Milrinone
A recent RCT found no difference in outcomes between dobutamine and milrinone in cardiogenic shock. Weigh the benefits of a catecholamine-sparing agent against the need for rapid titration and possible concomitant renal failure. [1]
Inotropic Support in SCM
Inotropic support for septic cardiomyopathy has not been shown to be beneficial, though it is not well-studied in overt sepsis-induced cardiogenic shock. [1]
⚠️ Negative inotropes should be avoided in cardiogenic shock. Pacing warranted if bradyarrhythmias contribute to hypoperfusion. [1]

Escalation Pathway [1]

Mark current step:

STEP 1Revascularize & Optimize
Restore perfusion to myocardium and end organs while treating the underlying cause. If ACS present, early invasive treatment and reperfusion is the mainstay. Target CI >2.2, MAP >65. [1]
STEP 2Inotrope ± Vasopressor
Dobutamine or milrinone (no outcome difference). Catecholamines are a bridge — weigh arrhythmogenicity and ↑myocardial O₂ demand. PAC strongly considered to guide decisions. [1]
If support inadequate despite maximal medical management → consider temporary MCS. Early initiation may confer survival benefit. [1]
STEP 3Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support
If hemodynamic support is inadequate despite maximal medical management, temporary MCS should be considered. Cannulation strategy, physiologic effects, and support level vary by device. Early initiation may confer survival benefit. [1]
Shock algorithms and multidisciplinary regional hub expertise enable time-sensitive, patient-centered decisions. [1]
DE-ESCALATIONRecovery or Bridge
If recovery on MCS: wean support. If no recovery: MCS can bridge to advanced therapies (transplant, durable device) or bridge to decision. If neurologic uncertainty or MSOF prohibits advanced therapy candidacy, goals-of-care discussions and consideration of withdrawal are warranted. [1]

🔧 Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support

If hemodynamic support is inadequate despite maximal medical management, temporary MCS should be considered. Cannulation strategies, physiologic effects, and amount of support vary based on the type of MCS initiated. Early initiation of MCS may confer survival benefit. [1]

When to Consider
Inadequate hemodynamic support despite maximal medical therapy. Given high mortality and the risks/costs of MCS, shock algorithms and multidisciplinary expertise at regional hubs allow early involvement of experts and time-sensitive, patient-centered decisions. [1]
Device Selection
Cannulation strategy, physiologic effects, and degree of support all vary by device type. Echocardiography confirms placement of MCS devices. [1]
Exit Strategy
If no recovery on MCS, it can bridge to advanced therapies (transplantation, durable support) or bridge to decision while further workup is obtained. [1]
⚠️ No exit strategy [1]: Neurologic uncertainty or multisystem organ failure prohibits advanced-therapy candidacy. Goals-of-care discussions should occur, and withdrawal of support considered if there is poor chance of recovery without an exit strategy on MCS.
Early initiation [1]: Early initiation of MCS may confer a survival benefit. Regional shock-team models support timely, expert-guided decision-making.

🔬 Pathophysiology of Hypovolemic Shock

Loss of intravascular volume — directly from whole blood loss or indirectly from extracellular fluid loss — leads to inadequate organ perfusion. All types decrease preload, creating a deficit in perfusion pressure so that both macrocirculation and microcirculation suffer. [1]

1
🩸 Volume Loss → ↓Preload
Whole-blood or extracellular fluid loss reduces preload → deficit in perfusion pressure → decreased tissue perfusion and triggering of an inflammatory cascade. [1]
2
🫀 Reflex Vasoconstriction (Tissue Level)
Hemorrhage → hypovolemia → reflex vasoconstriction → decreased blood flow to vital organs → multisystem organ failure in severe cases. [1]
3
🌐 Endothelial Injury (Vascular Level)
Local Thrombus Formation
Platelet activation → platelet–thrombin plug at the injury site [1]
Glycocalyx Shedding
From ↑catecholamines + oxygen debt → fibrinolysis & autoheparinization → coagulopathy [1]
Capillary Leak Syndrome
Glycocalyx shedding + leukocyte–endothelial interactions [1]
Compounded Coagulopathy
Decreased platelet number & activity; soft-tissue injury accelerates it [1]
4
⚗️ Cellular Failure & Inflammation
Acute ↓RBC quantity → inadequate O₂ delivery → anaerobic metabolism → lactic acid, inorganic phosphates, oxygen radicals → acidosis. Dying cells release DAMPs/alarmins → innate immune activation → systemic inflammatory response. [1]
Compounding factors in trauma [1]: Hypothermia and acidosis found in trauma patients exacerbate active bleeding. Soft-tissue injury in traumatic hemorrhagic shock contributes to and likely accelerates coagulopathy, possibly through mechanisms that worsen tissue hypoxia.
⚠️ Lab nuance [1]: Elevated lactate and base deficit are characteristic, but acid-base balance depends on the underlying cause (e.g., persistent vomiting can cause metabolic alkalosis). Hemoglobin/hematocrit are usually severely decreased — unless hemoconcentration is present, in which case they are increased.

📊 Classification of Hypovolemic Shock

Etiology-Based Classification [1]

Hypovolemic shock is divided by the nature of volume loss. Traumatic hemorrhagic shock is more common than nontraumatic causes of blood loss. [1]

CategorySubtypeExamples
Extracellular Fluid LossExternal (insensible)Open abdominal procedures, hyperthermia, respiratory system losses [1]

⚠️ Why the trauma distinction matters [1]: Soft-tissue injury increases the likelihood of disruption of blood–endothelium interactions and may accelerate coagulopathy through mechanisms that exacerbate tissue hypoxia.

Diabetes Insipidus — A Profound Sensible Loss [1]

DI is a defect in production (central) or utilization (nephrogenic) of ADH. Both types release large volumes of dilute urine; if untreated, patients are at risk for hypovolemic shock. Insensible losses alone are estimated up to 800 mL/d via skin, stool, and the respiratory system. [1]

💊 Medical Management

"Stop the Bleed" — Definitive Source Control [1]

Successful treatment ultimately depends on definitive correction of the underlying cause, plus the ability to temporize with balanced resuscitation and vasopressors in the interim. [1]

Definitive Bleeding Control
In the hospital, definitive bleeding control is the priority, alongside adequate restoration of euvolemia via balanced resuscitation. [1]
STOP THE BLEED Campaign
ACS initiative educating laypersons on hemorrhage-control tactics; improved confidence/willingness to render aid and increased tourniquet use after rollout. [1]
Thromboelastography
An excellent tool for monitoring clot formation that can help guide transfusion decision-making. INR/PT/aPTT may be elevated and fibrinogen decreased. [1]

Fluid, Blood Products & Vasopressors

Avoid Aggressive Crystalloid
En route to hospital, it is preferable to avoid aggressive crystalloid resuscitation to avoid clotting-factor dilution, hemodilution, and consequent exacerbation of hypoxia. [1]
Balanced Transfusion (1:1:1)
Blood products transfused in ratios resembling whole blood — usually 1:1:1 PRBC : FFP : platelets. Massive transfusion protocols at this rate improve morbidity and mortality of trauma patients in hemorrhagic shock. [1]
Vasopressors — Norepinephrine First-Line
If fluid alone is insufficient to restore perfusion (SBP <80 mmHg), temporize with vasopressors. Norepinephrine is suggested first-line — sympathomimetic with splanchnic venoconstriction shifting blood volume centrally → ↑venous return and CO. [1]
Phenylephrine Caution
Phenylephrine, though often used, may have opposing effects on the splanchnic pre-portal and hepatic veins → decreasing venous return and consequent downstream effects. [1]

Escalation Pathway [1]

Mark current step:

STEP 1Recognize & Assess Volume
Elevated lactate and base deficit are characteristic. Assess volume responsiveness: PPV (sinus rhythm, appropriately ventilated), CVP trends interpreted with ventilator settings, IVC ultrasound through the respiratory cycle. Pre-renal injury markers: ↑BUN/creatinine/urine osmolality, FENa <1%. [1]
STEP 2Source Control + Balanced Resuscitation
Definitive bleeding control is priority. Restore euvolemia with balanced resuscitation — 1:1:1 PRBC:FFP:platelets via massive transfusion protocol. Avoid aggressive crystalloid (dilution, hemodilution, hypoxia). TEG-guided product selection. [1]
STEP 3Vasopressors (Temporizing)
If fluid/blood is insufficient (SBP <80 mmHg), temporize with vasopressors. Norepinephrine is suggested first-line — splanchnic venoconstriction shifts volume centrally → ↑venous return and CO. [1]
Phenylephrine may oppose this via splanchnic pre-portal/hepatic vein effects → ↓venous return. Vasopressors are a bridge to definitive hemorrhage control. [1]
DE-ESCALATIONRestore Euvolemia & Wean
Once bleeding is controlled and euvolemia restored via balanced resuscitation, wean vasopressors. Reassess H&H (may be falsely elevated with hemoconcentration), lactate/base deficit, and coagulation status. [1]

🔬 Pathophysiology of Obstructive Shock

A physical impediment of blood flow in the cardiovascular system. The least common shock type, constituting about 2% of all shock states. Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and PE comprise the majority of cases. [1]

1
🚧 Mechanical Impediment
Any obstruction that restricts venous return, prevents diastolic filling, or severely elevates afterload can result in obstructive shock. Categorization centered on effects on ventricular preload and afterload is most intuitive. [1]
2
🧭 Two Mechanistic Categories
Impaired Preload / Diastolic Filling
Tension pneumo/hemothorax, tamponade, SVC syndrome, mediastinal tumors, massive PE (effect on LV), abdominal compartment syndrome, auto-PEEP [1]
Elevated Afterload
Massive PE (effect on RV), severe pulmonary hypertension, aortic occlusion, coarctation [1]
3
📉 Low-Output Physiology
Intravascular volume and cardiac contractility are usually preserved, yet CO, stroke volume, and MAP fall. SVR and CVP are usually increased; wedge pressure varies by etiology. [1]
4
⚠️ Compensation Then Collapse
Initially symptoms may be nonspecific (dyspnea, chest pain) and perturbations limited to compensatory tachycardia and tachypnea. For patients in extremis, diagnosis is clinical and treatment should not be delayed for diagnostics. [1]
⚠️ Exam clues [1]: Physical examination frequently reveals jugular venous distention, abnormal auscultatory findings, and cool extremities. Therapies most often consist of pharmacotherapy followed in quick succession by percutaneous or surgical relief of the obstruction.

🔍 Etiologies & Treatment

Tension Pneumothorax

Pathophysiology: Trapped air accumulates in the pleural space, increasing intrapleural pressure above atmospheric → ipsilateral lung collapse and mediastinal compression with contralateral shift. Most common in trauma, ARDS, and mechanically ventilated populations; a simple pneumothorax can convert to tension physiology if a one-way valve exists between alveolus and pleura. [1]

Clinical Signs
Cyanosis, tracheal deviation, decreased breath sounds, percussion hyperresonance, subcutaneous emphysema. On ventilator: elevated peak/plateau airway pressures with decreasing lung compliance. [1]
POCUS
Higher sensitivity than chest radiography (79% vs. 40%), similar specificity (98% vs. 99%). Findings: absent lung sliding and lung pulse, presence of lung point, barcode sign on M-mode. [1]
⚠️ Pneumothorax developing during mechanical ventilation progresses to hemodynamic instability in up to 70% of cases. TP is a clinical diagnosis — treatment should not be delayed to obtain imaging. [1]
TREATMENTDecompression
Emergent placement of a large-bore needle into the anterior second intercostal space in the midclavicular line, followed by a percutaneous small-bore thoracostomy catheter. [1]

Cardiac Tamponade

Pathophysiology: Restriction of cardiac filling from elevated intrapericardial pressure (blood, clot, pus, chyle, or gas). Compensatory ↑systemic/pulmonary venous pressures + tachycardia. As LV/RV diastolic, right atrial, and wedge pressures equalize, the Y-descent fades; with progression, pulsus paradoxus results from ventricular interdependence. [1]

Classic Exam
Muffled heart tones, JVD, systolic inspiratory variation >10 mmHg (pulsus paradoxus). ECG: microvoltage and electrical alternans with large effusions. [1]
Echocardiography
Diastolic collapse of the RV (highly specific), systolic collapse of the RA (early sign), plethoric IVC with hepatic flow reversal, respiratory variation of mitral/tricuspid inflow velocities. [1]
⚠️ Post-cardiac-surgery tamponade [1]: Requires high suspicion. Because the pericardial sac is often left open to the pleural spaces, a localized clot on one chamber causes an insidious picture — gradually worsening hypotension, low CO, increasing filling pressures, and escalating inotrope/pressor support, often without classic pressure equalization. TEE is useful but may miss regional clots.
TREATMENTPericardial Decompression
Evacuation of the pressurized pericardial content — percutaneous or surgical. [1]
TEMPORIZEWhile Awaiting Definitive Treatment
Enhance venous return (volume administration or leg elevation); promote chronotropy/inotropy and systemic venous pressure with epinephrine boluses; reduce intrathoracic pressure by limiting tidal volume and PEEP. [1]

Massive Pulmonary Embolism

Pathophysiology: PE results in obstructive shock when ≥2 lobar arteries and at least 50–60% of the vascular bed are affected, elevating pulmonary vascular resistance. Massive PE = hypotension with SBP <90 mmHg for >15 minutes. [1]

🌀 The RV Spiral of Death

Acute RV afterload triggers a self-reinforcing cascade [1]

1
↑ RV Afterload
≥2 lobar arteries and 50–60% of vascular bed obstructed → elevated pulmonary vascular resistance [1]
2
RV Chamber Enlargement & Myocardial Failure
Acute elevation of RV afterload leads to chamber enlargement and myocardial failure [1]
3
↓ LV Early Diastolic Filling & Preload
The enlarged, failing RV decreases early diastolic filling and preload of the LV [1]
4
↓ Stroke Volume & Cardiac Output
Reduced LV preload produces a reduction in stroke volume and cardiac output [1]
⚠️ The vicious cycle: Falling cardiac output reduces perfusion to the already-strained, failing RV — without relief of the obstruction, the spiral converges on circulatory collapse. [1]
Risk Stratification
Altered mental status, hypotension, elevated troponins, and BNP mark increased mortality/adverse outcomes. PESI / simplified PESI (sPESI) aid risk stratification and therapy selection. [1]
POCUS (rule-in)
High specificity, low sensitivity; relies on indirect evidence: RV dysfunction (TAPSE, S′, 60/60 sign), septal flattening in systole, RV:LV size, McConnell's sign (acute RV strain, not PE-specific). Thrombus-in-transit is the only pathognomonic finding. CT pulmonary angiography is the gold standard. [1]
FIRST-LINESystemic Thrombolysis
Patients with hypotension, shock, myocardial dysfunction, and low risk of bleeding should undergo systemic thrombolytic therapy. [1]
ALTERNATIVESCatheter-Based / Surgical
Catheter-based thrombus removal for high bleeding risk or failed systemic thrombolysis. Surgical embolectomy may also be of benefit. [1]
PERT: Because therapies frequently depend on local expertise, standardization of in-hospital care is recommended. Pulmonary Embolism Response Teams improve quality, efficiency, and multidisciplinary communication, resulting in improved outcomes. [1]

📈 General Obstructive Approach

Mark current step:

STEP 1Recognize
Nonspecific early symptoms (dyspnea, chest pain) + compensatory tachycardia/tachypnea. Look for JVD, abnormal auscultation, cool extremities; SVR and CVP usually increased. For patients in extremis, diagnosis is clinical. [1]
STEP 2Temporize (Pharmacotherapy)
Therapies most often consist of pharmacotherapy followed in quick succession by percutaneous or surgical relief of the obstruction. [1]
STEP 3Relieve the Obstruction
Etiology-specific: needle/thoracostomy decompression (TP), pericardial evacuation (tamponade), systemic thrombolysis / catheter removal / surgical embolectomy (PE). This is the definitive treatment. [1]
REASSESSConfirm Resolution
Reassess hemodynamics after obstruction relief — CO/SV/MAP should recover as the mechanical impediment resolves. A single patient may transition from one shock state to another, so continual re-evaluation is necessary. [1]
